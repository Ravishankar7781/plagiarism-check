Urban Beekeeping and the City Garden

Rooftop hives have spread across many large cities over the last fifteen years. Honeybees kept on city roofs often produce more honey than hives in the countryside, because parks, street trees and balcony gardens offer a long and varied flowering season. Researchers in Paris and London have measured hive weights through the summer and found steady gains from April until late August.

The picture is not entirely positive. When too many hives are placed in one district, managed honeybees can compete with wild bees for the same limited supply of pollen and nectar. Several ecologists now argue that cities should plant more flowers before they add more hives, since a crowded neighbourhood leaves solitary bees with less food.

Beekeepers in cities also face practical problems. Hives must be inspected every week in spring to prevent swarming, and a swarm landing on a busy pavement can frighten passers-by. Many councils now ask new beekeepers to complete a short training course and to register each colony with the local authority.
